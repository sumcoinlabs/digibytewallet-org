SpaceX's 15th Starship test flight is scheduled to begin from Starbase in South Texas on Friday, November 13, 2026. The flight plan is unusually ambitious: recover the Super Heavy booster soon after launch and, after a much longer mission, attempt to catch the Starship upper stage at the launch site.

**This is a prelaunch briefing, not a report that either catch has succeeded.** The actual liftoff time, flight profile and recovery decisions can change for safety, weather or technical reasons.

## When is Starship Flight 15 scheduled?

The planned launch window opens at **5:45 p.m. Eastern time (22:45 UTC)** on November 13, according to [Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-launching-historic-double-catch-starship-test-flight-today-watch-it-live). The FAA's [operations advisory](https://www.fly.faa.gov/adv/adv_spt) separately lists Starship Flight 15 with a November 13 launch window and a November 14 backup opportunity. Flight windows are targets, not guarantees of launch.

Reuters reported that Starship 42 and the upgraded Super Heavy Booster 22 were being prepared at SpaceX's Starbase launch site. The vehicle is intended to test more of the transportation system's reusable design rather than demonstrate a routine commercial service.

## What is the double-catch attempt?

SpaceX has previously used its launch tower's mechanical arms to catch returning Super Heavy boosters. Flight 15 would take that approach farther by attempting to recover the spacecraft as well. The upper stage has a very different return profile because it travels at orbital speeds and experiences extreme heat during atmospheric entry.

The booster is expected to return to Starbase within minutes of launch if the flight proceeds as planned. The spacecraft is intended to remain in orbit for roughly 22 hours before a planned return on Saturday, November 14. The approach, reentry and eventual catch all depend on favorable technical and safety assessments.

## What else is on the mission plan?

[Space.com reports](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-launching-historic-double-catch-starship-test-flight-today-watch-it-live) that the mission plan includes deploying **26 Starlink V3 satellites** and keeping the upper stage in flight long enough to assess its orbital and thermal performance before the proposed catch. Any satellite deployment, orbit count or return must be confirmed by post-launch reporting before being described as completed.

SpaceX can choose a controlled ocean splashdown instead of a catch if its flight rules or conditions do not support the attempted recovery. A safe abort of the recovery plan would not necessarily mean every other objective failed.

## Why this test matters

Successfully recovering both parts of a large launch system could reduce hardware loss and enable more frequent reuse. But a single demonstration would not by itself establish airline-like turnaround rates, verified commercial reliability or readiness for crewed lunar missions. Those outcomes require repeated safe flights and additional engineering work.

The mission also connects to a broader test program for transporting satellites, cargo and eventually people beyond Earth. The key technical milestone to watch is the upper stage's controlled return and whether the catch is attempted at all.
