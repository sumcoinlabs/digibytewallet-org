FAA Orders Nationwide Ground Stop After Air Traffic Control Communications Failure

November 13, 2026 · 22:09 UTC · DigiByte Wallet newsroom

The FAA ordered a nationwide halt to departures on November 13, 2026, following communications problems at air traffic control centers.

The Federal Aviation Administration ordered a nationwide ground stop for departing flights on Friday, November 13, 2026, after serious air traffic control communications problems disrupted operations. The agency cited exceptional safety circumstances as the reason for the unusual restriction.

The announcement affected aircraft awaiting departure across the United States. It did not mean that every aircraft already in the air was ordered to land immediately. The FAA said international operations would be managed individually, and the duration of the restriction remained a developing matter when this report was prepared.

What happened to U.S. flights?

According to Reuters, the FAA instructed departing traffic to remain on the ground amid communications difficulties involving air traffic control. The agency had also halted operations in Chicago shortly before the nationwide measure was reported.

The Associated Press reported that regional centers used to coordinate flights between airports experienced communications problems. Such centers play an essential role in keeping air traffic safely separated and directing aircraft through busy airspace.

A national ground stop is an exceptional response. A technical problem at a major component of the air traffic system can affect airports far beyond the area where the issue first appears. Airlines then need to reposition planes and crews after the immediate restriction ends, which can extend delays.

Was this the FAA's NOTAM system?

There is an important distinction between an air traffic control communications disruption and the 2023 failure of the Notice to Air Missions, or NOTAM, system. As of this report, the cause of Friday's communications problem had not been confirmed as a repeat of the earlier NOTAM failure. Describing the event simply as a confirmed NAS database outage would go beyond the publicly established facts.

The incident again raises questions about resilience and modernization in a safety-critical national infrastructure network. The FAA's daily air traffic report directs travelers to its operational status resources for changing conditions.

What travelers should do

  1. Check the airline's flight-specific status rather than assuming a national announcement applies equally to every route.
  2. Monitor the FAA's National Airspace System status for current restrictions and delay programs.
  3. Allow extra time for knock-on delays even after departure restrictions are lifted.
  4. Use the airline's official app or website for rebooking or other passenger instructions.

What remains unknown

The exact technical cause, the full duration, and the final number of affected departures were not confirmed in the sources available when this article was written. This is a developing report and should not be treated as live flight guidance.
